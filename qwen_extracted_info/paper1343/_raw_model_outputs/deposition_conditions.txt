 the films. This is in contrast to the results obtained for the two transition metal
nitrides where the same material phase (monoclinic HfNx and cubic TiNx) was formed at all
surface orientations. This suggests that the effect of ion energy on material phase formation is
not uniform across all materials. This could be due to the different bond energies of the materials
as discussed earlier. The results also suggest that the use of substrate biasing during PEALD can
be an effective method for enabling topographically selective deposition on 3D substrates. This
could be of particular interest for applications such as microelectronics, where the ability to
selectively deposit different materials on different regions of a 3D substrate is highly desirable.
CONCLUSIONS
The results obtained in this work demonstrate that substrate biasing during PEALD can be an
effective method for tuning a wide range of material properties of oxide and nitride thin-films.
The implementation of substrate biasing during PEALD can provide additional parameters for
tuning material properties, such as ion energy and dose, which can be used to control the
properties of the growing film. Furthermore, the implementation of substrate biasing during
PEALD can enable the formation of crystalline materials at low temperatures where PEALD
without biasing typically yields amorphous films. This eliminates the need for specific substrate
surfaces to deposit high temperature crystalline materials at low temperatures, thereby

enabling the use of more versatile substrates. The results also show that the application of
substrate biasing during PEALD can lead to the formation of sub-stoichiometric metal oxide
films by reducing the oxygen content in the films. This could be of particular interest for
applications such as resistive nonvolatile memory devices. Additionally, the results suggest that
substrate biasing during PEALD can be used to enable topographically selective deposition on
3D substrates, which is highly desirable for applications such as microelectronics. The results
obtained in this work also demonstrate that the application of substrate biasing during PEALD can
be used to control the properties of the growing film by modifying the ion energy and dose of
higher energy ions. This can be achieved by varying the magnitude and duration of the applied
bias during the plasma exposure step. The results further suggest that the application of substrate
biasing during PEALD can be extended to multistep PEALD processes, such as during the third
step of a three-step PEALD process, to enable more complex film structures. The results also
suggest that the application of substrate biasing during PEALD can be used to modify surface or
sub-surface regions of the substrate, enabling in-situ surface functionalization. These results
highlight the potential of substrate biasing during PEALD for enabling advanced material
processing and the development of new applications in the field of thin-film technology.
