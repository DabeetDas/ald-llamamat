For samples with 2 nm HfO2, the XPS As3d spectrum remains free of As2O3 and As2O5 peaks even after extensive air exposure. This further indicates that a 2 nm HfO2 layer fully covers the GaAs substrate and prevents its re-oxidation in air, in agreement with TOFSIMS. XPS indicates no interfacial layer (no AsOx or GaOx peaks) between GaAs and HfO2 for HfCl4/H2O ALD. This implies that the native oxide, present before ALD, has been consumed by the ALD process. The thermal ALD Al2O3 growth process from (CH3)3Al and H2O is a pure ligand exchange-based process, in which CH3 groups from TMA are exchanged for OH from H2O, releasing CH4 as reaction product. In the plasma-enhanced ALD (PE-ALD) process, the CH3 ligands undergo a combustion-like process with the O radicals formed in the remote plasma. We studied the PE-ALD process on an HCl-cleaned as well as on a S-treated surface, whereas the thermal process was applied on native oxide and on the HCl-cleaned surface. We approached this problem in a different way, in that we started from detailed molecular dynamics simulations in which we let the b2(2 /C24) GaAs surface (built up within the local density approximation (LDA) of density functional theory) react with molecular O2, starting in different positions (on top of the As dimer, in between the As dimers, and deep in the trough between the rows), and this both at room temperature and at 680 K. In a first stage, the approaching oxygen molecule is attracted by the electronic charge localized in the dimer As lone pairs. Subsequently, the dangling molecule is stabilized by binding to the neighboring atoms, either by forming a peroxyl link to a Ga atom, or by cleaving the neighboring dimer As–As bond and forming two O–As bonds. At increasing temperatures, the thermally excited vibrations of the O atom help in breaking the Ga–As bonds to form Ga–O–As bridges with an energetic gain on the order of 1 eV per bond. After these first steps of O chemi-sorption and diffusion (the formation of the deeper-lying Ga–O–As complex), we let the oxide layer grow further by offering more and more O. The volume expansion of the growing oxide layer creates tensile stress at the interface, which is released by the emission of Ga atoms from the lattice into the oxide, hereby creating vacancies in the lattice close to the interface, which obviously leads to unsaturated bonds of the neighboring atoms. The highly ordered reconstructed (2 /C24) surface becomes distorted during the oxidative metal–oxide deposition, leading to high concentrations of vacancies in the GaAs lattice just beneath the interface.
